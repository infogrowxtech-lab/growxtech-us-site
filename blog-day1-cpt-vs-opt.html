Day-1 CPT vs OPT: What's Actually Different, and the Real Risk

September 24, 2026 · 6 min read · Growx Tech IT

Day-1 CPT sounds like a shortcut to working sooner. The mechanics are real, but so is a trade-off most marketing pages leave out: a year of full-time CPT can quietly end your OPT eligibility.

The short answer: CPT (Curricular Practical Training) and OPT (Optional Practical Training) are both forms of authorized F-1 work, but they're structured differently. CPT is tied to one specific employer, authorized by your school, and normally requires a full academic year of enrollment first. OPT is authorized by USCIS, lets you work for any employer in your field, and doesn't require a job offer to apply. "Day-1 CPT" refers to programs, mostly newer or lesser-known schools, that offer full-time CPT starting from a student's very first term, skipping the usual one-year wait.

What CPT actually requires

Per DHS's Study in the States guidance, standard CPT eligibility requires a student to have been "lawfully enrolled on a full-time basis for at least a full academic year." There's a specific exception: graduate programs whose curriculum requires training from the start can authorize CPT earlier, this is the legitimate basis some graduate programs use, and it's different from a blanket "Day-1 CPT for everyone" policy. CPT also requires the training position to be secured before authorization, and it's employer-specific: the authorization is tied to one job, not open to any employer the way OPT is.

The trade-off nobody puts on the landing page

Here's the detail that matters most: one full year of full-time CPT (more than 20 hours a week) eliminates a student's eligibility for OPT entirely. Part-time CPT, 20 hours or less per week, doesn't carry this penalty. So a program that markets "work full-time from day one" as a selling point can, over a year, cost a student their entire post-completion OPT authorization, the same OPT that everything else on this blog (the 90-day clock, the STEM extension, cap-gap) assumes you're going to use.

This is the reason USCIS and immigration attorneys scrutinize Day-1 CPT programs more closely: they're not illegal by definition, but they're disproportionately associated with schools that have faced accreditation and legitimacy questions. The mechanism (CPT before one year, for graduate programs that require it) is real and legal. Whether a specific school's Day-1 CPT offering is legitimate is a school-by-school question, not a blanket answer.

DHS Study in the States: Curricular Practical Training

CPT vs OPT, side by side

  • Who authorizes it: CPT is authorized by your school's DSO in SEVIS. OPT is authorized by USCIS, which issues an Employment Authorization Document (EAD).
  • Job offer required first? CPT: yes, you need the position secured before authorization. OPT: no, you can apply and search after approval.
  • Employer flexibility: CPT ties you to one specific employer. OPT lets you work for any employer in a related field.
  • Timing: CPT happens during your program, while you're still enrolled full-time. OPT is typically used after your program ends (post-completion OPT), though a pre-completion version exists.
  • Effect on future eligibility: A full year of full-time CPT removes your OPT eligibility. CPT itself has no day-count "unemployment clock" the way OPT does, since you're required to have a position before you start.

What this means if you're choosing a program

If a school is advertising full-time work authorization from your first day, ask directly: is this graduate-program CPT tied to a curriculum requirement, or a blanket Day-1 CPT policy? And ask what happens to your OPT eligibility if you do a full year of it full-time. If the answer is vague, that's the answer. This is exactly the kind of question worth putting to your DSO or an immigration attorney before you enroll, not after.
