Canada Just Doubled How Long a Low-Wage Work Permit Can Last: What Changed on November 6

November 16, 2026 · 6 min read · Growx Tech IT

Effective November 6, 2026, Employment and Social Development Canada doubled the maximum duration of a low-wage Labour Market Impact Assessment from one year to two, which in turn raises the longest work permit IRCC can issue under that stream. Here's exactly what changed, why it's a different rule than the high-wage LMIA exemption we covered last month, and who it actually affects.

What Changed on November 6

Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) raised the maximum duration of a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) filed under the Temporary Foreign Worker Program's low-wage stream from one year to two years, effective for applications submitted on or after November 6, 2026. An LMIA sets the ceiling on how long the work permit tied to it can run, so in practice this means an employer using the low-wage stream can now sponsor a worker for up to two years on a single approved LMIA instead of having to reapply after just one. The change applies going forward only; LMIAs that were already approved under the old one-year limit are not retroactively extended.

This Is a Different Rule Than the One We Covered Last Month

It's worth being precise here, because Canada has made several LMIA-related announcements in the past few weeks and they're easy to conflate. In late October, Canada removed the LMIA requirement entirely for certain high-wage job offers, including software engineer and developer roles, as part of an Express Entry change that awards bonus points instead. That change affects skilled tech hires specifically. The November 6 change is unrelated and sits on the opposite end of the wage spectrum: it extends, but doesn't remove, the LMIA requirement for the low-wage stream, which by definition covers roles paid below the provincial or territorial median hourly wage. If your job offer qualifies as high-wage under Express Entry's definition, the November 6 change doesn't apply to you at all; if it's a lower-wage role, the LMIA is still required, it can just now cover a longer period once approved.

Myth worth retiring: a longer LMIA doesn't mean the low-wage moratorium lifted. Canada's freeze on processing new low-wage LMIAs in metro areas with unemployment of 6% or higher is still in place and still covers 30 census metropolitan areas, including Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal, Calgary, Edmonton, and Halifax. A small number of occupations, including primary agriculture, construction, food manufacturing, hospital and residential care roles, certain in-home caregivers, and, as of November 6, early childhood educators, are exempt from that moratorium. Being exempt from the moratorium means the application gets reviewed, not that it's automatically approved; the standard LMIA criteria still apply. Workers and employers in those 30 cities, applying for a non-exempt low-wage role, still can't get a new LMIA processed at all right now, regardless of the new two-year maximum.

Why This Doesn't Change Much for Most Skilled Tech Candidates

For the audience reading this looking for an IT role in Canada, specifically software, data, cloud, DevOps, or similar roles, this particular change is less directly relevant than it might first appear, precisely because those roles are much more likely to qualify as high-wage and therefore fall under the Express Entry LMIA exemption from late October rather than the low-wage stream this change touches. The low-wage stream is more commonly used for roles like food service, retail, hospitality, and certain caregiving and agricultural positions. If you're a software developer or data engineer with a Canadian job offer, the practical question to ask your prospective employer isn't about this November 6 change at all, it's whether your offer clears the high-wage threshold for Express Entry purposes in the first place, since that determines which set of rules actually governs your path.

A Second Change Came Alongside It

One other procedural change is worth knowing about if you have an in-Canada work permit application waiting on an LMIA decision. On October 28, 2026, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) removed the fixed holding period for concurrent work permit processing. Previously, IRCC held an eligible application for up to 90 days (a limit that had itself been raised from 60 days back in August) while waiting on the LMIA decision, and could refuse the application as incomplete if that window closed first. Officers are now instructed to keep waiting for the final LMIA decision with no fixed deadline, which removes one specific way an application could be refused purely on a clock running out rather than on its merits. It's a smaller change than the two-year maximum, but it affects a similar population of applicants and removes a real source of unnecessary refusals.

What's Still Just a Proposal

It's also worth separating what's actually in effect from what's still on the drawing board. Beyond the three changes above, Canada has floated a further package of Temporary Foreign Worker Program reforms targeted for March 1, 2027, including a cap limiting low-wage temporary foreign workers to 15% of an employer's workforce and a shift toward occupation-based access rather than the current geography-based moratorium. Those measures are not in effect, and reporting indicates they depend on provincial governments signing on; federal officials, including the minister responsible, are still in discussions with provincial counterparts about them. None of that changes anything about your job search today, but it's useful context if you see headlines about a "15% cap" and assume it's already binding. As of November 16, 2026, it isn't.

What This Means If You're Job Searching in Canada Right Now

The practical takeaway is to figure out which stream actually applies to your situation before reacting to any single headline. If you're targeting IT roles in Toronto, Vancouver, or another major Canadian metro and your offer is high-wage, the relevant recent change is the LMIA exemption under Express Entry, not this one. If your offer genuinely falls under the low-wage stream, the two-year maximum is a real improvement in stability, but it doesn't help you at all if you're applying for a non-exempt role in one of the 30 cities still under the processing moratorium, since the LMIA still won't be processed regardless of how long it could eventually last.
