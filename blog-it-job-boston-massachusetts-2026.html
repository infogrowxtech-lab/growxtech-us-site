How to Get an IT Job in Boston in 2026

October 12, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

Boston's tech market sent two conflicting signals in early October: a national ranking showing real strength, and a major local layoff. Here's what the data actually says, and how to position yourself either way.

Boston's tech market sent two very different signals in the first half of October 2026. One national ranking says the region just got stronger. One local company's layoff announcement says the opposite. Both are true at once, and understanding why matters if you're actually job hunting in IT around Boston and Cambridge right now rather than just reading the headlines.

Boston climbed to No. 7 in CBRE's 2026 tech talent ranking

CBRE's "Scoring Tech Talent 2026" report, an annual index of North American metros by the size, growth, and concentration of their tech workforce, moved Boston up two spots to No. 7 overall, with an index score of 62.88. On AI employment specifically, Boston ranked 8th nationally with 27,494 AI-specialty tech workers as of June 2026, behind the San Francisco Bay Area, New York, Seattle, Washington D.C., Toronto, Dallas-Fort Worth, and Los Angeles/Orange County, but ahead of Chicago and Atlanta. That's a meaningfully larger AI-specialty workforce than most of the metros Growx covers regularly, and it reflects Boston's long-standing strength in biotech, healthtech, and research-adjacent computing, not just pure software engineering.

Then HubSpot announced 660 layoffs, 7% of its workforce

On October 6, Cambridge-based HubSpot told employees it is cutting roughly 660 jobs, about 7% of its workforce of more than 9,000. CEO Yamini Rangan framed the move as a shift from building software "Hubs" toward organizing product teams around customer outcomes, with fewer management layers. Notably, she was explicit that this wasn't an AI-efficiency story: "This is not driven by AI-related efficiencies," she wrote, adding, "this is not simply a cost-cutting exercise." Affected employees are getting 20 weeks of base pay (up to 30 weeks with tenure), continued health benefits, six months of career-transition support, and to keep their laptops and home-office equipment.

One company's layoff isn't the same thing as a shrinking market. HubSpot's cuts are real and worth taking seriously if you work there or in a similar SaaS reorg, but they're a single-company restructuring story, not evidence that Boston tech is contracting broadly. The same week HubSpot announced cuts, the region's own state labor data showed Boston-area jobs dipped 1.2% month over month in August, while CBRE's independent, much larger dataset showed the metro's tech talent base climbing in the national rankings over the same year. Both numbers are real; they're just measuring different things on different timeframes.

What this actually means if you're searching in Boston right now

If you're one of the roughly 660 people affected by HubSpot's reorg, the practical first move is the severance and transition-support window you were given: six months of career-transition services is a real runway, and 20 to 30 weeks of pay buys you room to be deliberate rather than desperate. The honest challenge is that other Boston SaaS and enterprise software employers are watching the same AI-productivity conversation HubSpot's CEO was careful to say wasn't the actual driver here, so a resume that speaks only to "ran a SaaS product team" without naming the specific systems, data, or AI-adjacent tooling you worked with is going to blend into a crowded pool.

If you're not affected by this specific layoff but you're job hunting in the Boston metro generally, the CBRE data is the more useful signal: a tech workforce with the 8th-largest AI-specialty employment base in North America has real, ongoing demand, concentrated in biotech-adjacent data roles, healthtech infrastructure, and the research computing that Boston's university and hospital ecosystem has always fed into. That's a different hiring pattern than Austin's engineer-heavy startup market or Dallas-Fort Worth's AI-adjacent financial-services hiring: Boston rewards candidates who can speak credibly to regulated, research-heavy environments, even in IT support, QA, or implementation roles that aren't themselves scientific.

Where the actual openings are concentrated

Boston's tech base has never been one dominant employer the way some smaller metros are. It's a mix of large, established software and biotech-adjacent companies (HubSpot among them, restructuring aside), a dense academic and hospital research pipeline that constantly needs research-computing and data-infrastructure support, and a startup layer that draws on both. For a job seeker, that means the Boston market rewards flexibility in how you frame your background: the same systems-administration or data-support experience that reads as "generic IT" elsewhere can read as "research computing support" in Boston if you frame it that way, and that framing genuinely matters to recruiters screening for a research-adjacent environment.

It's also worth knowing that Boston's AI-specialty concentration, while real, trails the very top markets by a wide margin: 27,494 AI-specialty workers is a fraction of the Bay Area's 98,699 or New York's 67,949. That's not a reason to avoid the market, but it does mean pure AI-research roles are more competitive relative to supply than the broader "Boston tech is booming" narrative might suggest. The stronger near-term opportunity for most candidates is in the roles that support AI and research infrastructure rather than build frontier models directly: data engineering, ML operations, QA for regulated healthtech products, and implementation or customer-success roles at growth-stage biotech-adjacent SaaS companies.

A practical checklist for the Boston market

  • Name the regulated-industry context explicitly. If you've supported systems touching healthcare data, clinical research, or financial compliance, say so directly; Boston recruiters screen for that experience more than most other metros do.
  • Don't assume a SaaS layoff means SaaS hiring stopped. HubSpot's reorg is about management layers and product structure, not a broad freeze; other Boston SaaS employers are still hiring for the outcome-focused roles HubSpot itself says it's reorganizing around.
  • Lean into research-computing framing if you have any of it. Even informal exposure to research data pipelines, lab systems, or compliance-heavy infrastructure is worth surfacing clearly on a Boston-targeted resume.
  • Watch the 60-day median time-to-fill nationally as a planning benchmark; Boston's more specialized roles often run longer than that, so build your search runway accordingly.

The bottom line

Boston's tech market in October 2026 is genuinely a market of two stories: a visible, real layoff at a major local employer, and an independent national ranking showing the metro's underlying tech talent base getting stronger, not weaker. Reading only one of those stories will give you the wrong plan.
