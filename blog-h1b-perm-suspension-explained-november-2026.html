The PERM Suspension at Microsoft, Infosys and 6 Other Firms, Explained: What It Means If You're on H-1B

November 16, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

On October 8, 2026, the U.S. Department of Labor suspended eight major employers, Microsoft, Adobe, and six IT outsourcing firms, from the program that starts most employer-sponsored green cards. Six weeks later, the department's inspector general told Fox News that more companies and schools could be added next. Here's exactly what changed, and what it does and doesn't mean if you're job hunting on H-1B right now.

What the Labor Department Actually Did on October 8

On October 8, 2026, the Department of Labor announced it had suspended eight employers from the Permanent Labor Certification program, known as PERM, the first formal step toward most employer-sponsored green cards (the EB-2 and EB-3 categories). The list covers two technology companies, Microsoft and Adobe, and six IT outsourcing and consulting firms: Cognizant, Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro, HCL Technologies, and Capgemini. Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling described the action as "suspending from the permanent labor certification program some of the largest IT outsourcing firms in the world," citing active federal investigations. Cognizant had actually been cut off first, in early September, when the department's inspector general froze its new PERM filings on its own; the October 8 action extended the freeze to the other seven companies at once. The announcement came at a press event with Vice President JD Vance and Attorney General Todd Blanche, and the eight companies together hold more than 230,000 H-1B approvals and at least 100,000 permanent labor certifications between them, which is part of why the move drew so much attention.

What's Actually Alleged, and What Isn't Proven

The companies are under investigation for possible visa fraud tied to the H-1B and PERM programs. At the announcement, Vance said Microsoft had laid off roughly 6,000 American workers over the prior year while gaining approval for about 6,000 H-1B visas and filing roughly 3,682 PERM applications in the same period, and he said some foreign workers had reportedly been paid tens of thousands of dollars less than American employees in comparable roles. Blanche said criminal charges against companies or individuals were possible if the investigations bear that out. Microsoft pushed back publicly, saying that about 80% of its H-1B filings that fiscal year were to extend or change the status of workers it already employed rather than to hire new ones, and that its compensation is among the highest in the industry. None of these competing claims has been tested in court, and the other six companies did not respond publicly to the specific allegations at the time of the announcement. The practices under examination across the broader investigation, which falls under the White House's Fraud Task Force, include allegedly fraudulent visa or green card filings, wage kickbacks where workers are said to have had to return part of their pay, "benching" where H-1B employees are allegedly kept on payroll without work or pay between assignments, and hiring patterns that allegedly replace American workers with lower-paid foreign ones.

What a PERM Suspension Actually Means, Mechanically

PERM is the Labor Department's permanent labor certification process, and it's the step before an employer can file Form I-140 with USCIS on a worker's behalf, which is itself the step before the worker can eventually apply for the green card. A suspended employer cannot file new PERM applications, and any of its pending PERM cases are put on hold rather than processed. That matters for timing because employers generally have to file the PERM application within 180 days of completing their recruitment and advertising steps; if that window closes while the company is suspended, the recruitment may have to be redone once filing resumes. It also matters for priority dates, since a worker's place in the green card line is normally set by the date their PERM case was filed, so a freeze can effectively cost time in that line for workers whose employer hasn't filed yet.

Myth worth retiring: a PERM suspension is not an H-1B suspension. H-1B work authorization itself is untouched by this action; employees of the eight affected companies can keep working under their current H-1B status. The real risk is narrower and more specific: many H-1B workers eventually need an approved I-140, which comes after PERM, to extend their H-1B status beyond the normal six-year limit, so a prolonged freeze can still matter even though no one's current visa has been revoked. Workers who already have an approved I-140 are in a meaningfully different position, since an I-140 approved for at least 180 days generally keeps its priority date and allows H-1B extensions in three-year increments regardless of what happens to the company's PERM program afterward.

What Changed on November 16

The story didn't end on October 8. Six weeks later, Department of Labor Inspector General Anthony D'Esposito told Fox News that "the visa system and these visas that are in place are all temporary" and that they "are not a method of doing business," adding that employers should be turning to H-1B only when they genuinely can't find a qualified American worker for the role. He said his office, working alongside Vice President Vance's White House Fraud Task Force, expects enforcement action to extend to more companies and to educational institutions, not just the eight already named. He framed the administration's position as protecting American workers rather than restricting legal immigration generally. No new company names or suspension dates were announced in that interview, so as of today the confirmed list is still the original eight, but D'Esposito's comments make clear the investigation is continuing rather than winding down.

What This Means If You're Job Hunting Right Now

If you're actively interviewing with any of the eight named companies, it's worth asking directly, and early, how the PERM freeze affects their sponsorship timeline for the role you're being considered for, since the honest answer may simply be "paused" for the time being. If you already work at one of them and have a pending PERM case, check with your employer's immigration counsel about where your specific case stands rather than assuming the broad headlines apply exactly to your situation; the 180-day recruitment and filing clock and existing I-140 approvals both create real exceptions to the general freeze. And if you're earlier in your search and weighing offers, treat the suspension as one factor among several, not as a reason to rule a company out entirely, since the freeze doesn't touch anyone's current work authorization and the Labor Department itself has said it can lift all or part of a suspension if the investigation resolves in a company's favor. What's clear from D'Esposito's comments is that this is an active, expanding area of enforcement, so it's worth checking for updates on any employer you're relying on for sponsorship before you finalize a decision based on last month's facts.
