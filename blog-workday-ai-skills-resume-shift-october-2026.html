Why Demand for “Basic” AI Skills on Your Resume Just Fell 25%

October 12, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

A new Workday report shows employer demand for basic AI-prompting skills has fallen 25% since January, while demand for hands-on, build-level AI skills rose 51%. Here's what the data actually says, and what to do about it.

A new report from Workday, released October 5, landed on a finding that cuts against a year of "learn AI or get left behind" advice: demand for basic AI skills in job postings actually peaked in January 2026 and has fallen 25% since. The skills employers are chasing now are a narrower, harder-to-fake category, and the gap between the two is exactly where a lot of job seekers' resumes are currently stuck.

What Workday's report actually measured

The Workday Global Workforce Report draws on several real data sources: a September 2026 survey of 6,001 employees and business leaders, a separate "AI@Work Pulse" survey of 5,944 workers run in August 2026, de-identified HR data from Workday customers with at least 250 employees, and de-identified job requisition data from roughly 550 enterprise employers using Workday Recruiting, covering September 2025 through July 2026. That last dataset is where the skills-demand numbers come from, and it's a meaningfully large, real hiring-activity sample rather than a self-reported opinion survey.

The headline split is between two categories the report doesn't formally define with rigid boundaries, but illustrates with clear examples: "basic" AI skills, meaning simple prompting and using AI tools as they come, and "hands-on" or "build" AI skills, meaning actually building AI tools, automating workflows, and AI engineering. Demand for the basic category rose through late 2025, peaked in January 2026, and has fallen 25% in the months since. Demand for the hands-on category rose 51% over the same September 2025 to July 2026 window.

Myth to retire: "put AI on your resume" was never one piece of advice, and it stopped being good advice for the basic version months ago. A line like "proficient with AI tools" or "used ChatGPT to improve workflows" is exactly the category of claim that's lost 25% of its pull with employers since January. What's gained ground instead is specific: did you automate a workflow, integrate an API, fine-tune a model, or build something that didn't exist before. The bar moved from "I use it" to "I can build with it," and most resumes haven't caught up yet.

Workday Global Workforce Report, October 5, 2026

The hiring market is also getting more crowded, not less

The same report found the median number of applicants per filled role rose to 69 in 2026, up from 58 a year earlier, while the time it takes to actually fill a role held steady at around 60 days. That combination, more applicants chasing the same average fill time, means each individual application has to work harder to stand out than it did a year ago. The increase wasn't even across industries: applicants per filled role rose 27% year over year in financial services and 40% in technology and media specifically, two sectors that overlap heavily with Growx's own candidate base. More than half of applicants in both sectors, 53% in financial services and 57% in tech and media, said AI tools are a direct reason they're applying to more roles than before, which is likely a meaningful driver of the volume increase itself.

Workday also found employers aren't primarily leaving roles open because they can't find any candidates: they report roles stay open mainly because candidates lack the specific skills needed, or because pay, location, or flexibility don't line up with what candidates want. That's a useful reframe if you've been applying widely without responses: the gap is more often a skills-match problem than a "nobody's hiring" problem, and the AI-skills data above is one concrete place to close that gap.

Companies aren't mainly cutting headcount over this, but the job is still changing

It's worth being precise about what the report does and doesn't say about layoffs. Only 28% of business leaders in the survey expect AI to reduce headcount, while 40% expect it to help them get more out of the employees they already have. That's a meaningfully different story than "AI is replacing jobs" headlines suggest; it's closer to "AI is changing what the same job requires." Internal moves to new roles fell at 57% of employers year over year, and nearly 4 in 10 employees said their company went through a reorganization or restructuring in the past year. Phil Willburn, Workday's VP of people systems, intelligence and support, summed it up directly: "Employees may not be changing jobs, but their jobs are changing around them."

There's also a confidence gap worth naming. 79% of workers in the survey said they know what skills they need to succeed, but only 66% said their employer actually helps them develop those skills, a 13-point gap between knowing what's needed and getting support to get there. If you're job hunting rather than currently employed, that gap is effectively yours to close on your own, which is exactly why specificity on a resume matters more than a general AI-familiarity claim right now.

What to actually do with this if you're job hunting

  • Replace generic AI-use claims with a specific, verifiable example. "Built a script that automated X using Y" beats "proficient with AI tools" because it survives a follow-up question in an interview.
  • If you don't have a build-level AI example yet, get one before your next application round. That doesn't require an engineering background: automating a repetitive reporting task, connecting two tools via an API, or building a simple internal workflow all count as "hands-on," not just "basic."
  • Expect more competition per role, not less, even in a slower-feeling market. 69 median applicants per filled role means differentiation matters more than volume of applications sent.
  • If a role has been open a while, don't assume it's dead. Workday's data suggests open roles are more often stuck on a skills or compensation mismatch than genuinely paused, so a strong, specific application can still land.

The bottom line

The data doesn't support "learn to prompt ChatGPT and you're covered" anymore, and it also doesn't support "AI is coming for your job" as the dominant story either. The real shift is narrower and more actionable: employers are rewarding people who can build and automate with AI tools, not just use them, and that's a gap most resumes can close with a specific example or two rather than a wholesale career pivot.
