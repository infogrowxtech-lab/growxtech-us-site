Canada Drops the LMIA Requirement for High-Wage Tech Job Offers: What It Means for Your Toronto or Vancouver Job Search

October 26, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

Starting November 3, a job offer in a high-wage occupation — including software engineer and developer roles — can earn Express Entry bonus points and skip the LMIA requirement entirely. Here's exactly what's changing, and what it means for your Toronto or Vancouver tech job search.

Starting November 3, 2026, a job offer in a high-wage occupation — a category that explicitly includes software engineers and developers — can qualify a candidate for Express Entry bonus points and help meet Federal Skilled Worker or Federal Skilled Trades requirements without needing a Labour Market Impact Assessment first. That's a real, dated policy change from IRCC, not a proposal, and it's specifically relevant if you're building a tech career and a permanent residence path in Canada at the same time. Here's what's changing, what it doesn't change, and what it means if you're job hunting in Toronto or Vancouver right now. Growx does not provide immigration or legal advice; this is a factual summary of a published policy change.

What's actually changing on November 3

IRCC announced a new Comprehensive Ranking System component called the Labour Market Integration Factor on October 16, 2026, taking effect November 3. It replaces the points-for-job-offers structure that was removed back in March 2025, but with a different design built around wage level rather than job title alone. Occupations are sorted into three groups based on how their national median wage compares to the national average: Group A (at least double the average), Group B (at least 1.5 times but under double), and Group C (at least 1.3 times but under 1.5 times). According to law firm MLT Aikins' summary of the announcement, software engineers and developers fall into Group B, alongside occupations like data scientists — meaning a genuine high-wage tech job offer in that group is now one of two routes to meaningful bonus points under the new factor.

The other route is Canadian work experience: candidates can earn up to 80 points for experience in a TEER 0–3 occupation plus up to 70 more if that experience was in a high-wage occupation, for a combined maximum of 150 points. A qualifying job offer alone, without that Canadian work history, can earn up to 100 points depending on which wage group it falls into. Candidates choose whichever route scores higher; the two don't stack. Either way, the practical headline for tech workers is the LMIA piece: as of November 3, a job offer used to meet the 67-point Federal Skilled Worker threshold, the Federal Skilled Trades minimum, or the Labour Market Integration Factor's job-offer route no longer needs to be backed by a positive LMIA, according to CIC News' reporting on the reform.

What this doesn't change: you still need an actual, genuine job offer — this reform doesn't create points for having none. Candidates must still select "positive LMIA" when updating their Express Entry profile even though one isn't legally required to qualify, which is a quirk of how the system currently records it. IRCC is also pausing Express Entry invitations while it recalculates scores under the new factor, a process it says may take up to two weeks. And this is one piece of a larger Express Entry reform IRCC has said won't be fully rolled out until sometime between April and October 2027 — more changes are still coming.

Why this specifically matters if you're job hunting in Toronto or Vancouver

Toronto and Vancouver are Canada's two largest tech hiring markets, and software engineering and development roles are exactly the kind of position this reform was built around. A candidate who lands a genuine, high-wage software engineering offer in either market now has a meaningfully stronger Express Entry case than they did a year ago, when job offers earned zero CRS points at all following the March 2025 change. That's a real shift in how much a single strong job offer is worth to someone building both a career and a permanent residence pathway in Canada, and it changes the calculus on whether it's worth pursuing a Canadian employer directly versus relying purely on work experience accumulated after arrival.

It also raises the stakes on making sure an offer is positioned correctly from the start. Whether an offer lands in Group A, B, or C under the new wage bands depends on the occupation's NOC classification and its documented median wage, not just the number on your own offer letter, so candidates negotiating or evaluating offers in Toronto's or Vancouver's tech markets have a new, concrete reason to understand exactly how a role will be classified before assuming it qualifies for the higher point bands.

What this means practically for your search right now

If you're actively interviewing for software engineering or developer roles with employers in Toronto or Vancouver, this reform is a reason to treat a confirmed, well-documented offer as more valuable to your overall immigration strategy than it was a few months ago — not a reason to change how you interview or negotiate day to day. The job search fundamentals are unchanged: a resume and LinkedIn profile that clearly show your technical experience, interview preparation that lets you speak concretely about your work, and a search strategy that targets employers actually hiring in these specific metros. If anything, a policy change that makes a real offer more valuable raises the cost of a weak application that never gets you the interview in the first place.

It's also worth remembering that Express Entry scoring details like these change on a timeline set by IRCC, not by any single employer or candidate, and the broader reform package is explicitly not finished. Treat this update as current, verified information about what applies right now, not as a prediction about where CRS scoring ends up in 2027.
