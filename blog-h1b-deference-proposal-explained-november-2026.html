DHS Wants to End H-1B Extension “Deference”: What the October 14 Proposal Would Change

November 2, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

On October 14, DHS published a proposal to delete the regulation that tells USCIS officers to defer to their own prior approvals when adjudicating an extension. Comments close November 13. Here's what deference actually means today, what the proposal would do, and what is and isn't decided yet.

If you're on H-1B status and due for an extension in the next year or two, a regulatory proposal published in mid-October is worth understanding now, not after it's finalized. It doesn't change anything about your current extension, and it may never take effect at all. But it targets a specific piece of the adjudication process that a lot of H-1B workers don't realize is doing quiet, important work in their favor: a rule called "deference."

What "deference" actually means today

Under 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), when USCIS reviews an extension petition that involves the same employer, the same job, and the same underlying facts as a petition it already approved, officers are instructed to defer to that earlier approval rather than re-litigating the case from scratch. In practice, that means a straightforward same-employer, same-role H-1B extension usually moves faster and draws less scrutiny than the original petition did, because the officer starts from the presumption that the earlier decision was correct.

That presumption isn't absolute. Officers can decline to defer if they find a material error in the earlier approval, a material change in circumstances or in the eligibility requirements since then, or new information that adversely affects eligibility. Deference also doesn't apply to a petition changing employers, or to an amended petition where the job duties, worksite, or wage level materially changed. This isn't a new or obscure policy, either: it traces back to an April 2004 USCIS memo, was rescinded in October 2017, reinstated in the USCIS Policy Manual in April 2021, and was formally written into regulation as part of the H-1B modernization final rule in December 2024.

What the October 14 proposal would actually do

The new proposal, filed under RIN 1615-AD26 and docket number USCIS-2026-0529 (91 FR 65119–65133), would delete 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) outright. It isn't limited to H-1B: the deletion would apply to every nonimmigrant classification that uses Form I-129 for an extension, including E-1, E-2, E-3, H-1B1, H-2A, H-2B, H-3, L-1, O, P, Q-1, R-1, and TN, in addition to H-1B itself. Under the proposal, an officer reviewing an extension would no longer start from a presumption that the prior approval was correct. DHS says officers could still consider a prior approval as part of the record, but the extension would get what amounts to a fresh, independent eligibility review each time, including re-examination of points like specialty occupation classification and the worker's qualifications that were already resolved in the original petition.

The comment period is 30 days from publication, not 60 as a few outlets initially reported, which puts the deadline at 11:59 p.m. Eastern on November 13, 2026. Comments have to be filed through regulations.gov referencing docket USCIS-2026-0529; mailed, emailed, or hand-delivered comments won't be accepted. After the comment period closes, DHS will review what it receives before deciding whether to move forward with a final rule, and no timeline for that decision has been announced.

This is not a denial rule, and nothing has changed for extensions filed today. The proposal doesn't create a new ground for denying an extension, and the burden of proof stays exactly where it already is, on the petitioner. The current deference rule at 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) remains fully in effect, and will keep governing every extension filed unless and until DHS finalizes this proposal and sets an effective date, which it has not done. What would actually change, in DHS's own estimate, is volume: the agency projects roughly 6,044 additional Requests for Evidence per year if the rule is finalized, and cases that draw an RFE take a median of 64 additional days to decide. That's a timing and administrative-burden risk, not an eligibility one.

Where this fits in a year of H-1B regulatory activity

This proposal didn't arrive in isolation. It's one piece of a crowded 2026 H-1B regulatory landscape that also includes the $100,000 entry fee under Proclamation 11069, which two separate federal courts have now blocked (we covered the first block in September and the second one in October), a separate proposed $103,265 cap-subject fee that's still pending after its comment period closed in September, Executive Order 14431's directive to weigh an employer's layoff history in sponsorship decisions, and a separate proposal to eliminate the 60-day grace period after employment ends. None of that is required reading to understand the deference proposal specifically, but it's useful context: DHS has multiple active rulemakings touching H-1B eligibility and adjudication at the same time, and they move on independent timelines.

What this means if your extension is coming up

Because the current rule is still in force, nothing about how to file your next extension changes today. But a few things are worth doing regardless of whether this proposal is ultimately finalized. Keep your own file of the documentation that supported your last approval, including the original job description, wage level determination, and any specialty-occupation evidence, rather than assuming your employer's immigration counsel has instant access to it years later. If your role, title, duties, or worksite have shifted at all since your last approval, even informally, flag that to whoever is filing your extension well before the filing window opens, since a change like that already falls outside deference under the current rule and would need to be documented either way. And if you're also managing a job search on H-1B status at the same time, be direct with prospective employers about your timeline rather than hoping the extension process stays invisible to them. Vague answers tend to read worse than a clear, confident explanation of where things stand.

The bottom line

The deference proposal is a real regulatory development worth tracking, but it is exactly that right now: a proposal, with an open comment period and no finalized rule or effective date. If it is finalized, the practical effect for most H-1B workers would show up as more Requests for Evidence and longer processing timelines on extensions that would previously have moved through with little friction, not a new path to automatic denial.
