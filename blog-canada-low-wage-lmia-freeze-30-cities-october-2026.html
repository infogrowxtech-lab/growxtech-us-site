Canada's Low-Wage LMIA Freeze Just Expanded to 30 Cities: What Changed on October 9

November 2, 2026 · 6 min read · Growx Tech IT

Employment and Social Development Canada updates its list of high-unemployment cities where low-wage work permits can't be processed every three months. The October 9 update added 6 more cities and removed 2, bringing the total to 30 of 41 census metropolitan areas. Here's exactly what the rule does, and doesn't, cover.

Canada's Temporary Foreign Worker Program has a rule that quietly refuses to process certain work-permit applications based on local unemployment data, and on October 9, the list of cities it applies to got noticeably longer. If you're following the Canadian job market, including for tech roles, it's worth understanding exactly what this rule does, because the headline number, 30 cities now restricted, covers a much narrower slice of the labor market than it sounds like.

The rule in plain terms

Service Canada can refuse to even process a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) application, the document most employers need before they can legally hire a foreign worker outside Express Entry, when three things are all true at once: the position is in the low-wage stream, meaning the offered wage sits below the province or territory's wage threshold; the job is located in a census metropolitan area (CMA); and that CMA's unemployment rate is 6% or higher at the time the application is submitted. This refusal-to-process rule has applied to applications submitted on or after September 26, 2024, and the government reviews and updates the list of affected CMAs every three months using the latest Statistics Canada Labour Force Survey data.

The wage threshold that separates the low-wage and high-wage streams is itself set per province or territory, at the provincial or territorial median hourly wage plus 20%. For LMIA applications received since July 17, 2026, that threshold sits at $36.92 an hour in Ontario, $38.40 in British Columbia, $37.50 in Alberta, and $36.00 in Quebec, among others. An offer at or above that line puts the position in the high-wage stream, which is not subject to the 6%-unemployment refusal rule at all.

What changed on October 9

The quarterly update that took effect October 9 added six cities to the restricted list: Halifax, Nova Scotia; Fredericton, New Brunswick; Kingston, Ontario; St. Catharines–Niagara, Ontario; Regina, Saskatchewan; and Lethbridge, Alberta. Two cities came off the list because their unemployment rates had fallen back below 6%: Kamloops and Chilliwack, both in British Columbia. That brings the total from 26 to 30 of the 41 CMAs that Employment and Social Development Canada tracks for this purpose, a net increase of four. The update covers applications submitted between October 9, 2026 and January 7, 2027, after which the list updates again on January 8.

Myth worth retiring: this doesn't mean Canada stopped issuing work permits, or that tech hiring is specifically affected. The refusal-to-process rule applies only to new low-wage stream LMIA applications submitted in a restricted CMA. It doesn't cancel or affect any work permit someone already holds, it doesn't apply outside census metropolitan areas, and it doesn't touch the high-wage stream at all. A handful of sectors and positions, including primary agriculture, construction, food manufacturing, hospitals, nursing and residential care facilities, and certain in-home caregiver roles, are also exempt from the rule regardless of location. Most skilled IT and software roles clear the high-wage threshold in the markets where they're actually hired, which means this specific rule rarely touches them directly.

Why this still matters if you're job searching in tech

Even though most IT roles sit in the high-wage stream and clear of the 6% rule, the pattern behind it is still useful context for a Canadian job search. The list of restricted CMAs has moved in both directions over 2026, lifted in some regions in the spring and expanded again now, which signals that Canada's foreign-worker policy is actively responding to local labor-market conditions rather than following one fixed, static list. For job seekers, that reinforces a practical point we've made before: an employer-sponsored route through the high-wage LMIA stream, or a PR-track pathway like Express Entry that doesn't require an LMIA at all, tends to be considerably more stable ground than relying on a low-wage work offer in a market where local unemployment is a live, moving variable. We covered the mechanics of the high-wage Express Entry route in more detail in our earlier piece on Express Entry and high-wage LMIA tech jobs, which is worth reading alongside this update if you're weighing your options between Toronto, Vancouver, or another Canadian market.

What to actually check before relying on an LMIA-backed offer

If you've received or are negotiating a job offer in Canada that depends on an LMIA, a few concrete things are worth confirming before you build your plans around it. First, ask whether the offered wage clears your province's high-wage threshold; if it does, this specific restriction doesn't apply to your case at all. Second, if the wage is below that line and the job is in one of the 30 currently restricted metros, understand that the employer's application may simply not be processed, not denied on the merits, but refused without a substantive review, until the next quarterly list update in January. Third, remember that this is genuinely a quarterly-moving target: a city that's restricted today could come off the list in January, and a city that's clear today could be added. None of this is immigration advice, and anyone navigating an actual LMIA-dependent application should confirm current status directly with Employment and Social Development Canada or a licensed immigration professional, since the list and thresholds can change again before this piece does.

The bottom line

Canada's low-wage LMIA freeze now covers 30 of 41 tracked metros, up from 26 after the October 9 update, but the rule is narrower than the headline suggests: it only touches new low-wage stream applications in specific high-unemployment cities, not the high-wage stream most skilled tech roles fall into, and not any work permit someone already holds.
