A Federal Court Just Vacated the Rule That Would Have Ended “Duration of Status” for F-1 and J-1 Students

November 23, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

A federal judge vacated DHS's rule replacing “duration of status” with fixed admission periods for F-1 and J-1 students on November 16, 2026. Here's what the rule would have done, why the court struck it down, and what it means for your status right now.

On November 16, a federal judge in Massachusetts struck down a DHS rule that would have replaced the decades-old “duration of status” framework for F-1 students, J-1 exchange visitors, and I-visa foreign media representatives with fixed admission periods and a new USCIS extension requirement. The rule never actually took effect — it had already been on hold since September — but the November ruling is the first final word on whether it survives at all. Here's exactly what the rule would have done, what the court said, and what it means if you're on F-1 or J-1 status right now. Growx Tech IT doesn't provide immigration or legal advice; this is a factual summary of a published court ruling, not guidance on your individual case.

What the rule would have changed

Under the “duration of status” system that's governed F-1 and J-1 visas for decades, a student or exchange visitor is authorized to stay for as long as they remain enrolled and in compliance with their program, without a fixed expiration date on their admission. DHS published a final rule on July 17, 2026, that would have replaced that system with fixed admission periods of up to four years for most F, J, and I nonimmigrants. Anyone who needed more time than that fixed period — a longer degree program, a delayed graduation, additional OPT time — would have had to file an extension-of-stay application with USCIS before their authorized period ran out, something D/S never required.

The timeline: blocked in September, vacated in November

The rule was challenged almost immediately in Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration v. DHS, filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts. On September 14, 2026, Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV issued an order postponing the rule under the Administrative Procedure Act while the case proceeded, meaning it never actually went into effect for anyone. On November 16, the same judge went further: ruling on cross-motions for summary judgment, he vacated the rule in its entirety, finding it “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, and otherwise not in accordance with law.”

Why the court struck it down

According to law firm summaries of the ruling, Judge Saylor's decision rested on several specific failures by DHS: the agency didn't adequately weigh the rule's costs against its stated benefits, didn't substantiate the fraud and national-security concerns it cited as justification, didn't meaningfully consider less burdensome alternatives such as expanded use of the existing SEVIS tracking system, and didn't respond to a large share of the public comments it received. The ruling also found that certain restrictions in the rule on changing academic programs or transferring schools exceeded DHS's statutory authority for F-1 students specifically. Because D/S is such a longstanding, foundational piece of how F and J status works, the court vacated the rule outright rather than sending it back to DHS to fix.

What this doesn't mean: that the fight is necessarily over. A vacatur is the strongest outcome a challenger can get at the district court level, but it isn't automatically the last word. DHS had already appealed September's preliminary injunction to the First Circuit before November's ruling came down, and that appeal was still pending as of this writing. Whether DHS also appeals the final judgment itself hasn't been publicly confirmed. An appeal, if filed, wouldn't reinstate the rule on its own — that would require a further order, such as a stay, from a higher court — but it does mean this isn't guaranteed to be the final chapter.

What this actually means if you're on F-1 or J-1 right now

Practically, nothing changes for you, and that's the point. Duration of status remains the law nationwide: there's no fixed end date attached to your admission, the academic flexibility D/S has always allowed — changing your major, your degree level, or your school — continues to be permitted, and no action is required on your part. OPT and STEM OPT eligibility and the rules governing them are unaffected by this ruling either way, since the vacated rule was about the admission framework itself, not post-completion work authorization. If you've been following this story since the rule was first proposed, the practical upshot of both the September block and the November vacatur is the same: D/S kept working exactly as it always has, the entire time.

How this fits into a year of change for F-1 students

This ruling doesn't arrive in isolation. 2026 has been a year of near-constant proposed and litigated change around F-1 and J-1 status: a separate proposal to charge schools a $70,000 fee for a student's first OPT period, ongoing litigation over new CPT guidance, and shifting rules around filing deadlines and extension procedures have all moved through the system in parallel with the D/S fight. None of those other items is resolved by November's ruling, and none of them is addressed by it either — the vacatur applies specifically to the fixed-admission-period rule and nothing else. If you've lost track of which proposal is in effect, which is blocked, and which is still just proposed, that's a reasonable position to be in right now; the pace of change this year has outrun most people's ability to track it casually, which is exactly why official sources matter more than ever.

What to actually do with this

If you're an F-1 student who's been timing a program change, a transfer, or your OPT application around a worry that your admission period might suddenly have a fixed deadline, that worry hasn't materialized and, per this ruling, the four-year fixed-period framework doesn't exist right now. That said, immigration rulemaking in this area has moved quickly and repeatedly in 2026, and a vacated rule with a pending appeal is a different thing than a rule that's been permanently abandoned by DHS. The most reliable way to track where this actually stands is your school's international student office or an immigration attorney, not a headline's framing of a court ruling, especially if DHS does appeal the final judgment or proposes a revised version of the same policy later.
