Companies Are Shifting AI Budgets Toward Cybersecurity and Training: What It Means for Your Next Certification

October 19, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

New CompTIA research shows enterprise AI has moved past the experimentation phase, and the budget is following governance, security, and training, not just new tools. Here's what that shift actually means for which certification is worth your time right now.

For the last two years, the enterprise AI story has mostly been about adoption: who's using it, how fast, and for which tasks. CompTIA's newest research, published October 16, says that story just changed chapters. The organizations it surveyed aren't asking "should we use AI" anymore. They're asking "how do we govern it, secure it, and staff it," and the budget numbers back that up.

That shift matters well beyond IT departments running their own AI rollouts. It's a direct signal about which skills and certifications are about to be worth more on the open market, and it points in a different direction than the "learn to prompt an AI tool" advice that's dominated job-search content for the past year.

CompTIA's read: AI adoption just entered a new phase

CompTIA's Corporate AI Adoption research found that roughly six in ten organizations are now moving from individual, productivity-level AI use toward enterprise-wide implementation. That's a meaningfully bigger commitment than letting employees use a chatbot for drafting emails: it means AI tools and agents are getting embedded into core business processes, which raises the stakes on everything from data handling to vendor risk.

Tellingly, when CompTIA asked what's actually slowing that rollout down, cybersecurity concerns came in as the second-largest adoption challenge overall, trailing only the harder-to-solve question of how to balance human employees with AI-powered tools and agents in the same workflows. Security isn't a side issue in enterprise AI anymore. It's one of the two biggest things standing between a pilot project and a company-wide deployment.

Where the money is actually going: governance, data protection, and training

The policy priorities CompTIA found among surveyed companies are almost unanimous: 98% cite risk management as a priority area for their AI governance work, 96% cite data protection and privacy, and 95% cite governance itself. Those aren't close calls. Nearly every organization moving AI past the pilot stage says it needs formal structure around how that AI is controlled.

On spending specifically, 48% of organizations say cybersecurity will be a key focus of their AI-related budget, and the exact same share, 48%, plan to invest in employee AI training. Those two categories are tied for where new AI dollars are actually headed, ahead of most of the flashier use-case spending that got attention over the past two years.

This is a survey of stated priorities, not a job-postings count. CompTIA's research describes what companies say they plan to invest in, not confirmed headcount or open roles. It's a genuinely useful signal about where demand is likely headed over the next several quarters, but it's one input, not a guarantee that a specific certification gets you an offer by itself.

What this looks like next to the broader AI-skills hiring data

This isn't an isolated finding. CompTIA's separate analysis of September 2026 job postings, released October 2, found that active postings requiring AI-related skills had climbed to 349,821, up roughly 32,000 from August alone and up nearly 282% since May 2024. Software, cybersecurity, and data skills were named as the specific categories leading that demand. Put the two reports together and a consistent picture emerges: employers aren't just buying AI tools, they're building out the governance, security, and training infrastructure around those tools, and the postings data shows that's translating into real, if gradual, hiring demand for the people who can do that work.

CompTIA's own framing is worth taking seriously here, because it cuts against the instinct to chase only the newest AI-specific credential. The research explicitly calls for companies to close AI and cybersecurity skill gaps "while strengthening foundational technology competencies," not instead of them. Seth Robinson, CompTIA's VP of research, put it this way: "AI safety, governance and workforce development are becoming competitive advantages rather than compliance exercises." That's a signal that the market is rewarding people who pair solid fundamentals with AI-adjacent literacy, not people who have only ever learned to use a specific AI tool.

What this means if you're picking your next certification

If you're deciding where to put your study time over the next few months, this data points toward a specific, practical lane: security fundamentals plus enough AI literacy to talk credibly about how AI systems get governed, secured, and deployed responsibly. A straight cybersecurity foundation (something like CompTIA Security+ or a comparable entry point) stays relevant on its own merits, since cybersecurity concerns are now explicitly slowing enterprise AI projects down. Layering AI governance or compliance-adjacent knowledge on top of that foundation, rather than swapping it out for a narrower "AI tools" credential, is what the data above actually supports.

It also means the "balance humans and AI in the same workflow" challenge CompTIA identified as the single biggest adoption barrier is itself a skill employers will pay for: people who can explain where AI should and shouldn't be trusted in a given process, and who understand the compliance and legal guardrails CompTIA says IT, security, legal, and compliance teams are now expected to build together.

  • Don't drop foundational IT or security certifications for AI-only ones. CompTIA's own research frames this as "both," not "instead of."
  • Learn to talk about AI risk and governance, not just AI tools. That's the specific gap this research says companies are funding right now.
  • Expect this to show up gradually, not overnight. Spending priorities and hiring plans move faster in surveys than they do in actual job postings.
  • Use the overlap. Cybersecurity, data protection, and AI governance keep showing up together across both CompTIA reports cited here, so a resume that speaks to more than one of them reads as more current than one that speaks to only AI tool usage.
