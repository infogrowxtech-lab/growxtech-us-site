How to Get an IT Job in New York City as a Recent Grad in 2026

October 19, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

New York has the country's largest tech workforce, and also one of its most crowded entry-level markets: a new report finds junior tech postings down 50% since 2022 while the city's graduate pipeline keeps growing. Here's what the data says, and how to actually stand out in it.

New York City has the largest tech workforce in the country, and it's also where a very specific 2026 problem is showing up in sharp relief: there are more computer science graduates coming out of the city's schools than there are entry-level tech jobs to absorb them. If you're a recent grad trying to break into IT in NYC right now, understanding that gap, and how to position around it, matters more than another generic "move to a tech hub" pep talk.

The entry-level squeeze is real, and it's documented

The Center for an Urban Future's September 2026 report, "Strengthening NYC's Entry-Level Tech Pathways in the Age of AI," found that entry-level tech job postings in New York City have fallen by roughly 50% since 2022. That's not a gradual dip. It's a halving of the starter-role market in under four years, happening at the same time the pipeline of candidates has grown substantially.

That pipeline growth is the other half of the story. CUNY computer science enrollment rose 146% between 2014 and 2024, from 4,639 students to 11,431. At the K-12 level, the share of NYC public school students taking computer science classes rose from 5.5% (about 54,000 students) in 2016 to 20% (more than 193,000 students) in 2024. The city has spent a decade successfully building a much bigger pipeline of CS-educated graduates right as the number of entry-level roles to catch them has shrunk.

This isn't unique to New York, but New York feels it acutely

Nationally, recent college graduates had an unemployment rate of about 5.6% and an underemployment rate of 42% as of the second quarter of 2026, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's ongoing tracking of the college labor market. Underemployment there means working in a job that typically doesn't require a bachelor's degree at all, not just a job outside your field. Four in ten recent grads nationally are in that position.

New York concentrates that pressure because it's simultaneously the biggest market and one of the most competitive. CompTIA's broader 2026 forecast still projects net US tech employment growth of around 1.9% for the year, meaning the overall tech jobs picture is not shrinking. The mismatch is specifically at the entry level, where NYC's huge, well-educated graduate pool is competing for a shrinking share of starter roles, while experienced roles and AI-adjacent specialist positions keep opening up elsewhere in the market.

"No entry-level jobs" doesn't mean "no jobs for new grads." It means the traditional junior-generalist role, the one where you learn everything on the job with heavy supervision, is getting rarer. Some employers, like mortgage-tech firm Valon, are now restricting new hires from using generative AI tools until managers confirm they can independently catch the tool's errors, which tells you what employers actually want proof of before they'll fully trust a junior hire.

What actually differentiates a candidate in this market

If the generalist junior role is shrinking, the practical move is to stop presenting yourself as a generalist. New York's tech employer base is unusually diverse: fintech, ad tech, media tech, healthtech, and a dense enterprise-software and consulting layer all hire in the city, and each of those verticals has specific, learnable context that a generic "I know Python and SQL" resume doesn't signal. A CUNY or any other NYC-area grad who can point to a project, internship, or coursework tied to one of those verticals (payments and compliance for fintech, HIPAA-adjacent data handling for healthtech, ad-serving systems for ad tech) reads as meaningfully more hireable than one who lists the same generic stack everyone else in the graduating class also lists.

The AI-skills point cuts both ways here too. Employers are increasingly wary of junior candidates who can only produce AI-generated work without being able to explain or check it, which is exactly the concern behind policies like Valon's. The stronger signal for a new grad isn't "I used ChatGPT to build this," it's being able to walk through what the AI-assisted code or analysis actually does and where it could be wrong. That's a specific, demonstrable skill, and it's one you can build and show in an interview even without years of professional experience.

A practical positioning plan for NYC specifically

  • Pick a vertical and say so explicitly. "Software developer" is a crowded label in a city with this many CS grads. "Software developer with fintech compliance coursework" or "...with healthcare data experience from a CUNY capstone" is a narrower, more memorable one.
  • Be ready to explain your own AI-assisted work line by line. Employers are specifically watching for candidates who can't do this; being able to do it well is a genuine differentiator right now.
  • Target the roles adjacent to entry-level, not just the entry-level label. Junior roles paired with a specific tool, platform, or compliance context (junior data analyst with SQL and a specific industry's data model, for example) face less direct competition than a bare "junior software engineer" posting.
  • Treat the 50% posting decline as a search-length signal, not a rejection of your résumé. A longer search in this specific market segment is a documented market condition, not necessarily a reflection of your candidacy.

It's also worth being realistic about timeline. A market where the entry-level posting count has been cut in half isn't one where a strong resume guarantees a fast outcome; it's one where a strong, specifically-targeted resume meaningfully improves your odds against a much larger applicant pool per opening. Budgeting for a longer search, while still applying consistently and following up on every lead, is a more accurate plan than expecting the first few weeks to produce an offer the way they might have in 2021 or 2022, when entry-level postings in the city were roughly double today's level.

One more thing worth knowing if you're weighing whether to stay in New York or widen your search: the city's 50% entry-level decline is specific to junior roles, not the overall tech market, and it doesn't mean every neighboring metro is doing better. Growx works with candidates across multiple US and Canadian tech hubs, and the honest comparison across most of them right now is "different mix of openings," not "obviously easier somewhere else." For most recent grads with family, school, or visa ties to New York, the better use of time is sharpening how you present yourself for the roles that do exist here rather than assuming relocation alone solves the entry-level squeeze.
