You Put AI Skills on Your Resume. Is Anyone Actually Seeing Them?

October 19, 2026 · 7 min read · Growx Tech IT

New ZipRecruiter data finds a big gap between job seekers claiming AI skills and actually surfacing them, while a separate study ties AI-written resumes to a sharp rise in unchecked inaccuracies. Here's what both findings mean for your next application.

Two numbers from recent survey data sit awkwardly next to each other. Job seekers are listing AI skills on their resumes at a steady clip, and employers say they're adjusting expectations because of AI, but very few of those AI-skills claims are actually landing where a recruiter or a screening tool is likely to notice them. At the same time, a separate study on how people are using AI to write resumes in the first place found a pattern worth taking seriously before you lean on a tool to fix either problem.

You probably listed your AI skills. Did anyone see them?

ZipRecruiter's Q3 2026 Job Seeker Confidence Survey, fielded in late July and August among more than 1,500 US job seekers, found that 69% say employer expectations have shifted because of AI, and 67% say they're already integrating AI into their routine work. On the resume side specifically, 43% of job seekers say they list AI competencies on their resume, but only about 10% give those skills prominent placement where a reviewer is likely to actually register them early.

That's a four-to-one gap between claiming a skill and surfacing it where it counts. Burying an AI-related skill in a long list at the bottom of a resume, rather than naming it in a bullet point tied to a specific result, is close to not claiming it at all, from a recruiter's perspective. The same survey found 29% of job seekers use AI to find jobs, 25% use it to draft resumes, and 26% use it for interview prep, so AI tools themselves aren't the gap. The gap is in how the resulting skills get presented once the resume is actually assembled.

The other side of this: AI-written resumes and what they cost people

Novoresume's Resume Report 2026, based on a May 2026 survey of 2,000 US adults, found that 42.6% of Americans used AI in some form the last time they updated their resume, and 27.1% have submitted a resume to a real job that was written almost entirely by AI without significant edits. That second number is the one worth sitting with if you're tempted to let a tool do the whole job.

The same report found that people who let AI write most or all of their resume admit to resume inaccuracies at nearly three times the rate of people who didn't use AI at all (74.8% versus 24.6%), and they're eight times more likely to say a resume lie actually cost them a job (25.2% versus 3.1% across all workers). Novoresume co-founder Andrei Kurtuy framed the risk directly: "AI can absolutely help you write a stronger resume, but it cannot be trusted to decide what is true about your career." His read on where it goes wrong: "The workers getting burned are the ones who paste in whatever the tool produces and hit submit."

The problem isn't using AI on a resume. It's not checking what it produced. AI tools are genuinely useful for structuring a resume, suggesting phrasing, and helping someone who's a strong candidate but a weak writer get their actual experience across clearly. The data above isn't an argument against using AI; it's an argument against treating its output as finished without verifying every claim it makes about you.

Novoresume, "The Resume Report 2026" (May 2026 survey, published June 15, 2026)

What ties these two findings together

Both studies point at the same underlying habit: treating a resume as something to generate and submit quickly, rather than something to deliberately construct. The 43%-claim-but-10%-surface gap on AI skills and the 27.1%-submit-with-no-edits figure on AI-written resumes both come from the same shortcut, skipping the step where you actually look at what's on the page and ask whether it represents you accurately and puts your strongest, most relevant material where a reader will actually see it.

There's a useful tell in the ZipRecruiter data here too: 63% of job seekers say they view AI as a threat to the value of their own skills, while at the same time most of them are using AI tools somewhere in their search. That tension is worth resolving explicitly rather than ignoring. If you've genuinely built or used AI tools in a professional context, that's a real, differentiating skill right now, not a generic add-on; the CompTIA data on tech job postings shows AI-related skill requirements up roughly 282% since May 2024. The way to capture that value isn't to add a line reading "familiar with AI tools" to a skills list. It's to describe, in a results-oriented bullet, what you specifically did with AI and what it changed.

A practical checklist before you submit your next application

  • Move your strongest, most specific AI-related accomplishment into a top bullet under a relevant role, not into a generic skills list at the bottom. Name the tool and the result, not just "AI-skilled."
  • Read every line an AI tool generated about you and verify it against reality. If you can't back up a claim in an interview, it shouldn't be on the resume, regardless of who or what wrote it.
  • Customize every submission. Novoresume's data found people who send the same resume everywhere have a far higher zero-interview rate than people who tailor each version; an AI draft is a starting point, not a final, reusable document.
  • Be ready to talk through any AI-assisted work in detail. Employers are increasingly screening for whether a candidate actually understands the work behind an AI-assisted result, not just whether they produced it.

Generational data in the same surveys adds a useful wrinkle. ZipRecruiter found Gen Z job seekers report the highest rate of expecting AI to affect their career path, and Novoresume found Gen Z is more than twice as likely as Boomers to have used AI to generate a resume and applies to roughly 22 jobs per search on average, versus about 9 for Boomers. Submitting more applications isn't automatically an advantage if more of them are unedited AI output; Novoresume's data found people who send an identical resume to every opening are roughly three times more likely to finish a search with zero interview invitations than people who tailor each one, regardless of how that resume was originally drafted.

None of this means an ATS or a recruiter can reliably "detect" AI-written content and penalize it on sight; neither study makes that claim, and most of the real risk comes from unchecked inaccuracies rather than writing style. The practical risk is narrower and more avoidable: a resume that reads like everyone else's because it was generated the same way, or one with a claim you can't actually back up, costs you a callback or an offer regardless of which tool produced it.
